Satisfiability.

- definition: a proposition is satisfiable if we can
    find a truth assignment that makes the statement true



- what kind of statement is satisfiable? what kind is not?
    satisfiable: tautology, contingent
    not satisfiable: contradictory




- is this satisfiable?

    (p ∨ ¬q) ∧ (q ∨ ¬r) ∧ (r ∨ ¬p)

p: T
q: T
r: T

this makes the entire statement true

to show a statement is satisifiable: 
    just need ONE truth assignment that makes it true 
    *could* fill in a truth table if you can't find one easily


to show a statement is NOT satisfiable: 
    need to fill in the WHOLE truth table 
    need to show it is classified as contradictory

Proving an argument.

*Premises* - pieces of information we are given (assume true)

*Conclusion* - something we are trying to show logically 
    follows from our premises.



An argument is of the form:

    IF all the premises really are true, 
    THEN the conclusion is true

premise1 ∧ premise2 ∧ ... ∧ premiseN → conclusion 

--> when is an argument invalid?
    promise broken
    all premises are true
    but conclusion is false
    considered invalid if this EVER happens for any truth assignment

--> When is an argument considered valid?
    not invalid 
    every truth assignment that made all the premises true 
        also made the conclusion true

Proofs with truth tables.

Suppose we know:

    - I am in the CS dept or I am in the ECE dept
    - I am not in the ECE dept
    - I can log in to the CS lab computers if
        I am in the CS dept

I want to prove:

    - I can log in to the CS lab computers
        and I am in the CS dept

--> How would we translate to propositional logic?

Propositional atoms:
    p: I'm in the CS department
    q: I'm in the ECE department
    r: I can log in to the CS lab computers


Translations:

Premises:
    - I am in the CS dept or I am in the ECE dept
        p ∨ q

    - I am not in the ECE dept
        ¬q    

    - I can log in to the CS lab computers if
        I am in the CS dept
        p → r


Conclusion:     - I can log in to the CS lab computers
        and I am in the CS dept

        r ∧ p

--> How to prove with a truth table?

Premises: 
    p ∨ q
    ¬q
    p → r

Conclusion: r ∧ p

valid: every truth assignment that made all premises true
    also made conclusion true

New idea: demonstrate conclusion follows from premises using
*natural deduction*

--> use a minimal set of deduction rules

--> for each logical operator
    - *introduction* rule:
        lets us introduce a claim with that operator

    - *elimination* rule:
        lets us extract pieces from a claim with
            that operator

--> apply these rules to create new claims that 
    logically follow
